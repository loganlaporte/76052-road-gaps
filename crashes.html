More traffic, the same roads, more crashes

The Road Gaps summary shows where 76052's roads haven't kept up with growth: a one-lane-each-way bridge on the main way out of Sendera, a 2-lane FM 156 through Haslet, and US 287 carrying nearly three times the traffic it did in 2010. This page adds the other half of the story. It uses TxDOT's own crash records to ask what happens on those same roads as they fill up.

The answer so far: crashes on the roads that stayed the same size have grown much faster than traffic. On US 287 inside the ZIP, the chance of a crash per mile driven has roughly tripled since 2019. At the Avondale-Haslet bridge interchange, crashes have nearly doubled. And on FM 156, crashes climb every time traffic nears what the road can carry.

Independent analysis by a 76052 resident. Crash data comes from TxDOT's Crash Records Information System (CRIS), which compiles Texas peace officer crash reports. US 287: crashes are outrunning traffic

If crashes only grew because more cars use the road, the two lines below would rise together. Through 2021 they roughly did. Since 2022 crashes have pulled far ahead. Traffic in 2025 was about 1.5 times the 2017–19 level. Crashes were almost 5 times higher.

The increase isn't in one spot. The stretch south of Avondale-Haslet through Haslet went from 3–7 crashes a year before 2020 to about 30 a year now. The Wise County end around the SH 114 interchange went from 6–8 a year to 31–37. The most common crash types are single vehicles leaving the road, sideswipes during lane changes, and rear-ends. Those are the crashes that grow when traffic is dense and speeds vary. Only a handful are marked as work-zone crashes, so construction doesn't explain the jump.

FM 156: crashes rise and fall with traffic

FM 156 is still one lane each way through Haslet. Each dot below is one year: how much traffic TxDOT counted in Haslet, and how many crashes happened on FM 156 inside the ZIP. When traffic climbed about 30% in 2021–22, to the level where the road reaches rush-hour capacity, crashes more than doubled. When traffic eased, crashes fell back.

Traffic vs. crashes, one dot per year

Correlation between yearly traffic and yearly crashes: 0.73 (1.0 would mean they move in lockstep).

How FM 156 crashes happen

The top type is a car hitting another that has slowed or stopped: the signature crash of a road that backs up. At rush hour it makes up 45% of FM 156 crashes, compared with 34% the rest of the day.

TxDOT's Dallas District lists the FM 156 widening in Denton County at $103 million with no funding and no construction date. In Haslet, TxDOT's Fort Worth District is still studying it. See finding B.

The bridge over US 287 carries one lane each way. Within about 350 m it meets the frontage-road signals, the Saginaw Blvd (Business 287) signal and FM 718. How crashes on the bridge's city approach happen

Left turns across oncoming traffic lead by far, and 63% of these crashes happen between 6 and 9 am or 3 and 7 pm. With two signals this close together, lines back up across the turn lanes and drivers turn through gaps.

    What this shows, and what it can't

    What the data shows

    • Crashes on these roads rose from about 66–101 a year (2016–19) to 172 in 2024 and 2025, and 2026 is on pace for more.
    • On US 287, crashes grew about 3.7 times while traffic grew about 1.4 times, comparing 2023–25 with 2017–19. The crash rate per mile driven nearly tripled.
    • On FM 156, crashes track traffic closely and jump when traffic reaches the road's rush-hour capacity.
    • At the bridge interchange, crashes roughly doubled, and most are rush-hour left-turn crashes.
    • The bridge is still one lane each way, FM 156 through Haslet is still one lane each way, and US 287 is still two lanes each way. The widenings that exist on paper are unfunded, still being studied, or stop short of the bridge.

    What it can't prove

    • This is a correlation. More people driving, new drivers who don't know the roads, more trucks and distracted driving all play a part, and a crash report doesn't say whether a wider road would have prevented it.
    • We can't say how many of the 31 deaths a new bridge or extra lanes would have prevented.
    • What traffic engineers do agree on is that crashes rise faster than traffic once a road runs near capacity. Stop-and-go queues, short gaps for left turns and lane changes in dense traffic all multiply conflicts. That's the pattern in these numbers.

    The question for TxDOT and the city

    Every year these projects stay unfunded, the roads carry more traffic and, so far, more crashes. People have been hurt at a rising rate on roads that are due for work but have no construction date. Residents deserve a clear answer on when that changes: for the Avondale-Haslet bridge, for FM 156 through Haslet and Denton County, and for US 287 through the ZIP.

    How this was built

    Crash records come from TxDOT's public CRIS query tool. Two queries were run for 2016 through September 25, 2026: every crash on US 287/US 81, FM 156, FM 718 and Business 287 in Tarrant, Denton and Wise counties, and every crash on Avondale Haslet Rd in Tarrant County. Crashes were kept when their map coordinates fall inside the Census boundary for ZIP 76052.

    Crash rates divide crashes by vehicle-miles: TxDOT's annual traffic count times 365 days times the road's length inside the ZIP (5.5 miles for US 287). Traffic figures and rush-hour capacity estimates come from the Traffic over time section.

    Limits of the data

    • About 4% of crash reports have no coordinates and can't be placed. They're left out, so the counts here are a little low, never high.
    • CRIS includes only crashes reported by police on a state crash form (usually one with an injury or at least $1,000 of damage). Minor fender-benders that nobody reported aren't counted.
    • 2016 figures for the city section of Avondale-Haslet look incomplete, so bridge comparisons start in 2017.
    • US 287 traffic comes from two count stations; the south end near FM 156 carries more traffic, so the rate there is somewhat lower than the ZIP-wide figure.
    • Hotspot names are the nearest cross street to the center of each cluster.

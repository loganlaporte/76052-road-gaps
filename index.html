Our roads haven't kept up with 76052

The ZIP grew from 15,995 people in 2010 to 26,675 in 2020, and satellite images show building hasn't slowed since. This page maps where the road network hasn't caught up: 4-lane roads that squeeze to 2, a one-lane-each-way bridge on the main road out of Sendera, rail crossings that trains block, and new neighborhoods served by rural roads. Everything here comes from public data, and each spot was checked on aerial imagery. Traffic counts are TxDOT's annual averages (AADT, vehicles per day).

Independent analysis by a 76052 resident using public data. The ZIP spans Tarrant, Denton and Wise counties, and every finding lists its county and who to ask. Not affiliated with TxDOT, the City of Fort Worth, the City of Haslet, any of the three counties, or BNSF.

The five biggest gaps

  1. The Avondale-Haslet bridge over US 287 is one lane each way, squeezed between a 4–5 lane road and the Saginaw signal. It's the main way out for more than 10,500 homes and carried up to 19,700 vehicles a day in 2024, likely more than it can handle at rush hour. Traffic J9
  2. FM 156 through Haslet is still one lane each way but carried 18,600 vehicles a day at its 2021–22 peak, right at rush-hour capacity, and about 16,000 in 2025. TxDOT is studying a widening with no construction date. B
  3. Avondale-Haslet crosses the BNSF tracks in Haslet at grade, and people have filed 197 reports of trains blocking it. J3
  4. New Northstar Pkwy crosses the tracks at grade just 48 m from the next intersection, while new subdivisions go up on both sides. J1
  5. A neighborhood of about 750 homes connects straight onto US 287, where there's no frontage road, into about 59,000 vehicles a day. G

Area J: choke points and odd intersections

Area J sits between two BNSF rail lines. Every road west to US 287 except one crosses the tracks at grade, and so does the main road east to Haslet. The one road that passes under the tracks is Barnwood Dr, off John Day Rd, and the direct road to it hasn't been built. Area J also spans all three counties: most of Sendera is in Tarrant, the north side is in Denton, and Northstar Pkwy's rail crossing (J1) and its dead end at US 287 (J6) are in unincorporated Wise County. Each intersection was checked on the newest high-resolution imagery (January 3, 2026, 0.34 m) and compared Sentinel-2 satellite passes from September 10, 2025 and September 15, 2026 to see what's been built since.

Traffic keeps climbing. The roads mostly haven't.

TxDOT counts traffic at fixed stations every year on state roads, and every few years on city roads like Avondale-Haslet. These are its published daily averages (AADT). To estimate rush hour, the daily count is multiplied by TxDOT's own rush-hour share for that road and the share heading the busy direction, then compared with what a lane can typically carry.

"Recent trend" is a straight line fitted to TxDOT's 2015–2025 counts at each station, which is gentler than compounding the growth. The bridge has only two counts (2019 and 2024), so its trend just continues that five-year change. "TxDOT growth rate" applies the yearly growth factor in TxDOT's own 2023 roadway inventory (1.4–1.9% a year on these roads). Actual growth has run far faster: US 287 at FM 156 averaged 7.8% a year from 2015 to 2025. On the recent trend, that stretch reaches about 94,000 vehicles a day around 2030, the design-year traffic TxDOT's inventory lists for it. Projections assume nothing changes: no new lanes, no new routes and no shift in where people drive.

How the rush-hour estimate works: busiest hour = daily count × TxDOT's peak-hour share (7–9% on these roads) × share in the busy direction (51–60%), from TxDOT's 2023 roadway inventory. Capacity is a planning rule of thumb: about 1,900 vehicles an hour of green light per lane with signals getting roughly 45% green time, and about 2,250 an hour per freeway lane, both reduced for trucks. TxDOT doesn't publish peak factors for Avondale-Haslet, so the bridge uses a typical 9% and 60%; the dashed line shows the result with gentler 8% and 55%. City roads are only counted every five years, and FM 156 counts dropped after 2022, possibly as new roads such as Haslet Pkwy took some traffic. Daily averages also hide the worst 15 minutes, crash days and trains blocking crossings.

What you can do

Tell TxDOT

US 287, its frontage roads, the Avondale-Haslet bridge and FM 156 are state roads. Two TxDOT districts split this ZIP. Ask that the Avondale-Haslet bridge and the FM 156 widening be funded, and name the spots on this page.

Report blocked crossings

Each time a train blocks Avondale-Haslet, Northstar Pkwy or Blue Mound Rd, file a report with the Federal Railroad Administration. These reports are the numbers on this page, and more reports build the case.

Speak at a council or commissioners court

Signals and Avondale-Haslet are city decisions. The Northstar rail crossing and its dead end at US 287 are in unincorporated Wise County, so that ask goes to the Wise County Commissioners Court. Public comment is usually open for about three minutes per person.

How to read this

A 2-lane arterial usually starts to break down somewhere around 13,000 to 17,000 vehicles a day, depending on signals, driveways and turn lanes. That's a rule-of-thumb planning range, not a TxDOT standard. So a lane drop matters most when the 2-lane side still carries that much traffic.

Neighborhood areas were built by grouping connected streets, counting the building footprints in each, and counting every place a street in that area meets an outside road. A detached home generates about 9.4 vehicle trips on a weekday (ITE's standard planning rate). Most fire codes, including IFC Appendix D, call for a second access road once a development passes 30 homes, unless the homes have sprinklers.

Limits of the data

  • TxDOT often lists city and county streets as 2 lanes by default, and some local counts are from 2019. New roads such as Haslet Parkway aren't in the 2023 inventory yet.
  • Not every OSM road has a lane tag. Short 4-lane flares at intersections were filtered out (both sides of a drop had to be at least 250 m long).
  • The aerial imagery predates current construction, so it shows conditions before the Avondale-Haslet work.
  • Only about 8% of neighborhood streets in OSM have a lane count, so a lane drop inside a subdivision could be missed. None turned up in the streets that are tagged.
  • Rail data comes from TxDOT's grade-crossing layer, which carries FRA trains-per-day, crash and blocked-crossing report counts. Some of its traffic counts are older than the homes around them.
  • Sentinel-2 has 10 m pixels. It shows new grading, pavement and roofs clearly, but not lane striping or signal heads.
  • "Homes" are building footprints within 70 m of a neighborhood street, with garages, sheds and commercial buildings removed when they're labeled. On acreage lots, unlabeled barns and shops inflate the count. In fast-growing tract neighborhoods the footprints run far behind: for area J, city address and appraisal records show about 1.6 times as many homes, so area J uses those records instead.
